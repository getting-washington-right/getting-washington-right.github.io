What your choices mean

    Assumptions and sources
    • Revenue baseline: the Economic and Revenue Forecast Council's September 2026 forecast of about $82.6B for 2027–29, including about $3.5B from the new tax on income over $1 million. Existing taxes excluding that tax: about $79.1B.
    • Spending baseline: about $85.6B, the midpoint of an estimated $84.6–86.6B cost to continue current services, based on legislative staff's four-year outlook. The split among categories is an estimate based on the budget's published shares (K-12 about 42%, Medicaid about 12%, other human services about 24%, debt service about 4%).
    • Sales tax: each 0.1 point of the 6.5% state rate raises about $0.27B a year (Department of Revenue estimate), so about $0.53B per two-year budget. Household cost assumes a median household spends about $26,700 a year on taxable purchases (estimate).
    • Capital gains (~$1.1B) and estate (~$1.4B) figures double FY2025 collections from the Department of Revenue.
    • Luxury home sales surcharge (1% above $3.025M): about $150M a year, based on a 2024 legislative fiscal analysis. Tax break closures: about $423M a year from Department of Revenue revenue alternatives (2024), which need re-scoring.
    • Lawsuit payouts: about $1.3B projected for 2027–29 (Department of Enterprise Services). Reform savings range is an estimate.
    • Food assistance penalty: under federal law, an error rate above 6% triggers state cost-sharing of more than $90M a year from October 2027 (DSHS), about $0.17B for the budget period.
    • Consequence estimates use rough unit costs: about $125,000 a year per teacher including benefits; about $3,000 a year in state cost per Medicaid enrollee, with roughly a dollar of federal matching money per state dollar (actual match varies from 50% to 90%); about $30,000 a year per long-term care or developmental disabilities client; about $12,000 a year per child in subsidized child care; about $120,000 a year per public safety position; about $10,000 a year per college grant recipient. All are estimates for scale only.
    • Rainy-day fund: about $1.0B available; using it is one-time money that does not fix an ongoing gap.
    • This tool shows scale and trade-offs. It is not an official fiscal analysis.

    About this tool

    This is an independent, non-partisan tool to help Washingtonians understand the trade-offs in the state budget. It is not an official state estimate, and the assumptions above explain every number.
